Every city generates thousands of tonnes of municipal solid waste every day, from households, shops, institutions, markets, hotels, hospitals, airports and industry. A waste segregation plant scientifically separates that mixed waste into recyclable and reusable fractions. Instead of sending everything to landfill, it recovers plastics, paper, metals and glass, diverts organic waste to composting, and prepares combustibles as refuse-derived fuel (RDF).

For municipal corporations, smart cities, industrial townships, airports, universities and large residential communities, an efficient segregation plant is the backbone of sustainable waste management.

## What is a waste segregation plant?

It is a facility where mixed municipal solid waste is separated by material type, size, weight, density and recyclability. Recyclables such as plastics, paper, cardboard, metals and glass are recovered; organic waste goes to composting or bio-methanation; combustible non-recyclables become RDF; and only inert rejects go to scientific landfill.

Modern plants combine manual inspection with automated equipment: conveyors, bag openers, trommel screens, magnetic separators, air density separators and balers.

## Why segregation matters

Segregation is the foundation of every successful MSW system. Without it, recyclables are contaminated, organic waste cannot be composted effectively and landfills fill faster. Good segregation:

- Reduces landfill dependency
- Improves recycling efficiency
- Makes compost production possible
- Enables RDF production
- Protects the environment and public health
- Conserves natural resources
- Supports regulatory compliance
- Creates employment
- Strengthens the circular economy

## The process in a 50–100 TPD plant

The aim of the workflow is to recover valuable resources, produce compost from biodegradable waste, make RDF from combustibles, and send only inert rejects to scientific landfill.

### 1. Collection

Mixed waste arrives from households, shops, markets, institutions and public places in compactor trucks, tippers, dumper placers, auto tippers and hook-loader containers.

### 2. Weighbridge and vehicle inspection

Every vehicle is weighed on arrival. Vehicle number, collection zone, arrival time, gross weight and net waste weight are recorded to track daily quantities and plant performance.

### 3. Tipping floor

Waste is unloaded on the tipping floor, where operators inspect it for excess moisture, hazardous and biomedical waste, bulky items, large stones, concrete and tree trunks. Removing these early protects the machinery downstream.

### 4. Feeding hopper

Loaders or excavators fill the feeding hopper, which provides a continuous, uniform flow, prevents overloading and reduces downtime.

### 5. Bag opening

Much municipal waste arrives in plastic bags. A bag opener tears them open without over-shredding the contents, exposing the waste for faster and more accurate sorting.

### 6. Primary conveyor

A primary conveyor carries the waste through each processing station, reducing manual handling and improving worker safety. Belt scrapers, emergency pull cords and side guards are standard.

### 7. Manual pre-sorting

Trained operators remove large plastic sheets, logs, tyres, fabric rolls, furniture, e-waste, batteries, gas cylinders and hazardous items before mechanical separation, protecting the trommel and air density separator from damage.

### 8. Trommel screening

The trommel, a rotating perforated drum, separates waste by size. The fine fraction (typically 0–80 mm) of organics, soil and sand goes to composting. The oversize fraction of plastic, cloth, rubber, paper, metals, glass and RDF material moves on for recovery.

### 9. Magnetic separation

A suspended magnet removes ferrous metals such as iron, steel, tin cans, nails and wire. This protects downstream equipment, improves RDF quality and produces saleable scrap.

### 10. Air density separation

Controlled airflow separates light materials (plastic film, LDPE, multi-layer plastic, paper, textiles, dry leaves) for RDF or recycling from heavy materials (stones, glass, bricks, ceramics, heavy plastics, metal pieces) for recycling or inert disposal.

### 11. Quality sorting

Workers sort the recovered material into clean categories: PET, HDPE, PP, cardboard, white paper, mixed paper, aluminium, copper, ferrous metal, glass, rubber and textiles. Clean, well-sorted material commands better prices.

### 12. Organic processing

The fine organic fraction is treated by windrow, aerobic or in-vessel composting, or by bio-methanation, producing compost, soil conditioner and, where applicable, biogas.

### 13–16. Plastic, paper, metal and glass recovery

Plastics are sorted by polymer and baled or shredded, with non-recyclables routed to RDF. Newspaper, corrugated board, office and kraft paper are baled for paper mills. Steel, iron, aluminium, copper and brass are recovered for recycling. Glass is cleaned and sent to recyclers.

### 17. RDF preparation

High-calorific combustibles such as textiles, paper residue, plastic film, laminates, rubber and foam are dried if required, shredded, screened and inspected to make RDF, which is widely used as an alternative fuel in cement plants.

### 18. Baling, storage and dispatch

A horizontal baler compresses recovered recyclables into dense bales, which are labelled, stored and dispatched to authorised recyclers, compost users and RDF consumers.

## Who needs a 50–100 TPD plant?

Municipal councils and corporations, smart cities, airports, industrial parks, educational campuses, defence establishments, tourism zones, integrated townships and large residential projects. A 50 TPD plant processes about 50 tonnes a day and a 100 TPD plant about 100; actual output depends on waste composition, moisture, source segregation and operating efficiency.

## Common challenges

- Mixed waste due to poor source segregation
- High moisture during the monsoon
- Contaminated recyclables and seasonal variation in composition
- Odour and dust
- Equipment wear and tear
- Inadequate worker training
- Fluctuating prices for recyclables

## Best practices

- Promote source segregation in homes and businesses
- Carry out regular preventive maintenance and calibration
- Provide proper PPE and train operators in safe handling
- Install dust suppression and odour control
- Monitor recovery rates and review monthly performance reports
- Keep storage areas clean and check quality before dispatch

## Frequently asked questions

**How does a segregation plant work?** Waste is unloaded, fed onto conveyors, screened in a trommel, separated by magnetic and air density systems, sorted by hand, and directed to recycling, composting, RDF production or landfill.

**What is an MRF?** A Material Recovery Facility is a plant that recovers recyclables such as plastics, paper, cardboard, metals and glass from mixed waste.

**What equipment does it use?** A feeding hopper, conveyors, bag opener, trommel screen, magnetic separator, air density separator, sorting conveyor, shredder and horizontal baler.

**Can mixed municipal waste be processed?** Yes. Modern plants are designed for mixed MSW, though source segregation improves recovery and material quality.

## In summary

A well-designed segregation plant turns mixed municipal waste into a set of clean, valuable streams: recyclables, compost and fuel. It reduces landfill dependency and supports a cleaner, circular economy.
